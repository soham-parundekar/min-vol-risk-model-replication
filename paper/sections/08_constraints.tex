\section{What the constraints buy}
\label{sec:constraints}
Over this sample the optimised replicas are more volatile than the heuristic
one, not less: 15.49\% to 15.73\% against 14.06\%. The programme in
\eqref{eq:qp} solves for minimum predicted variance at every review and still
ends the period with more realised variance than a rule that sorts on trailing
volatility and divides. That is worth stating plainly before asking what the
constraints are for, because the obvious justification for the extra machinery
is not available. The general form of this is familiar:
\citet{demiguel2009optimal} report optimised portfolios losing out of sample to
a rule that does no estimation at all. What is specific here is that both
portfolios are published indices with stated rules, so the comparison is
between two designs someone chose rather than between a design and a
straw man.

What the constraints deliver is not less risk but control over where it sits.
The two designs concentrate in opposite places, and the contrast is sharp
enough to survive the measurement caveat that follows it.

\subsection{Industry exposure}
The sector band binds. Across the reviews examined, the optimised replica's
largest active industry weight sits at the five per cent bound at every date,
ranging from $+5.02\%$ to $-5.12\%$ as the binding industry changes from
consumer non-durables to manufacturing. That is a statement about a constraint
doing its job rather than an empirical discovery: the band is five per cent and
the optimiser spends it.

The heuristic has no such bound and the result is not subtle. Its largest
active industry weight is an underweight to business equipment, and it grows
monotonically across the six reviews examined, from $-31.97\%$ to $-47.62\%$.
By the last review the index is running close to a fifty-point underweight to a
single industry, not by decision but as the arithmetic consequence of ranking
on volatility during a period when one large industry was persistently the
most volatile. A rule that sorts on volatility alone will concentrate wherever
the quietest industries happen to be, and it will keep concentrating for as
long as that persists.

Two qualifications. The reviews available for this comparison are the six most
recent for each portfolio, and because the optimised index reviews
semi-annually while the heuristic reviews quarterly, those six span 2023-11 to
2026-05 for one and 2025-05 to 2026-08 for the other. The windows do not
overlap, so this is a contrast between a binding constraint and its absence,
not a matched comparison over common dates. Second, the optimised figure
reaches $5.12\%$ rather than exactly $5.00\%$ at two reviews. The sector band
was never the bound the relaxation ladder loosened, so this is not a logged
relaxation; it is the residual of the drop-and-resolve pass described in
Section~\ref{sec:design}, where removing sub-floor holdings can leave the
remaining names unable to satisfy the band exactly. Twelve basis points on a
five hundred basis point bound does not change the comparison with a
four-thousand basis point drift, but it is a consequence of the heuristic
floor and belongs in Section~\ref{sec:limits} rather than hidden in a rounding.

\subsection{Stock-level concentration runs the other way}
The optimiser is the more concentrated portfolio at the level of individual
holdings. It holds between 74 and 88 names on average depending on the
estimator, with a largest position near 2.0\% to 2.8\% and an effective number
of holdings between 61 and 68. The heuristic holds exactly 100 by construction,
with a largest position of 1.29\% and an effective number of 98.7, which is
close to equal weighting because inverse-volatility weights across a
pre-filtered low-volatility set do not vary much.

So the two designs are each concentrated, in different dimensions, and a
single-number comparison of concentration would mislead in whichever direction
it was taken. The optimiser accepts stock-level concentration in exchange for
industry-level diversification; the heuristic accepts the reverse without
having chosen to. The difference is that one of them is the product of stated
bounds a reader can inspect, and the other is an emergent property of a sorting
rule that mentions neither industries nor position sizes.

\subsection{What binds}
Table~\ref{tab:horserace} records which bound actually constrained the
programme. Across 29 reviews the structural specification required a loosened
bound twice; the other estimators between five and seven times. In nearly every
case the bound that had to give was the turnover cap, not the sector band, the
weight cap or the minimum holding. Turnover is the binding constraint in this
replication, and the sector and style bands, which receive most of the
attention in descriptions of how such indices are built, are rarely the
operative limit.

Two reviews, one under the sample estimator and one under the beta-exempt
variant, exhausted the ladder entirely. Following the methodology, those
reviews produce no trade at all and the previous weights are carried forward,
after which the book drifts with prices. That is the mechanism behind the
largest observed active sector weight in the study, 5.39\% under the sample
estimator, and it is a faithful reproduction of what the published rules
prescribe rather than a failure of the replication.
