\section{Data, and what free data costs}
\label{sec:data}
Every input is free. Daily prices, dividends, splits and volume come from a
public quotation service; point-in-time book equity and shares outstanding from
the SEC's XBRL company-facts interface; industry codes from the same
registrant's EDGAR submissions, mapped to twelve Fama--French groupings;
attribution factors from the Fama/French Data Library and AQR's public
betting-against-beta series; and point-in-time index membership from a public
repository whose commit hash the acquisition script records so a run is
reproducible against a vintage \citep{secedgarapi,frenchdatalibrary,aqrbab}.
Prices begin in 2008 so that a 252-day beta and a 12-1 momentum signal have
history before the first exposure date. Exposures begin in January 2010.

That start date is a data decision rather than a modelling one. Mandatory XBRL
tagging phased in across filers between 2009 and 2011 \citep{secedgarapi}, so
point-in-time share counts are substantially incomplete for index members
before 2010. Because the parent index is rebuilt from those share counts and
every beta in the study is measured against that parent, incomplete coverage
degrades not one input but the benchmark the whole model is estimated against.
Beginning in 2010 costs six months of sample and removes a stretch in which the
benchmark itself is unreliable.

\subsection{Point-in-time discipline}
Fundamentals are keyed on the date a fact was \emph{filed}, never on the fiscal
period it describes. The distinction is the difference between a book-to-price
ratio an investor could have computed and one that incorporates a filing made
weeks later. XBRL reports both dates, which makes the correct behaviour
available and the incorrect behaviour easy, and a test asserts that every value
the pipeline returns for a date traces to a fact filed on or before it.

\subsection{Three ways free data misleads}
Each of the following produces results that look clean. None announces itself.

\textbf{Survivorship.} The quotation service keeps almost nothing for companies
that were acquired or went through bankruptcy. Of 899 tickers that passed
through the index over the sample, 242 return no usable history at all, which
is 27\% of the names the study would otherwise have used. The
consequence is not that the sample is smaller but that it is biased in a known
direction: a parent index rebuilt from the survivors outperforms the one that
actually existed. We measure the bias rather than assert it, by rebuilding the
cap-weighted parent and comparing it against the market ETF over the same days.
The rebuilt parent compounds at 14.78\% against 14.21\%, a gap of 0.51
percentage points a year, at 1.50\% tracking error and a correlation of 0.9962.
The gap is widest in 2020 at 2.54 points and 2025 at 2.35, which is the
expected pattern: bankruptcies cluster, so the survivorship advantage is
concentrated in the years where the missing names did worst.
Figure~\ref{fig:survivorship} shows it year by year.

\textbf{Ticker reuse.} A symbol released by a delisting is reassigned years
later to an unrelated company, and the service returns the new company's
history under the old symbol without comment. Requesting \texttt{ABI}, an index
member from 1996 until its removal in November 2008, returns a company whose
price history begins in June 2025, sixteen years after the member of that name
ceased to exist. Joined naively, a recent listing's returns are stitched onto a
constituent that no longer exists. The defence is to intersect every price
series with the window in which that symbol was an index member and
to treat a series whose history begins after membership ended as a different
company. Thirty-three symbols are caught this way, among them several that a
reader would recognise. Three more are dropped for having too few observations
inside their membership window to support anything.

\textbf{Filer errors served through unchanged.} Registrants occasionally tag a
share count with the wrong magnitude, and EDGAR passes the error along. One
filer reported $3.670\times10^{14}$ shares in a filing of October 2016 against
a contemporaneous count near $3.47\times10^{8}$, an error of six orders of
magnitude; another reported $1.981\times10^{11}$ later the same month against
a count near $1.93\times10^{8}$. Left alone, a single such fact gives one stock
a market capitalisation larger than world output, and since the parent is
cap-weighted, the parent becomes that stock.

The two cases need different defences, which is why both exist. The first is
isolated, and is caught by comparing each share fact against a rolling median
of its neighbours on a split-adjusted basis, the split adjustment being what
keeps a genuine split from tripping the filter. The second repeats across
consecutive filings, so its own neighbours agree with it and the rolling median
passes it through; it is caught instead by a cross-sectional check against the
median capitalisation of that week's index. A filter that handles only the
first kind would leave the second in place and look like it had worked.

A fourth trap is specific to adjusted price series. The quotation service
back-adjusts the close for splits, so multiplying a historical share count from
a filing by a historical adjusted close understates capitalisation by the
cumulative split factor. The share count is as-reported and the price is
as-adjusted, and the two live in different units. We reconstruct the split
history separately and convert reported share counts into current units before
they meet a price.

\subsection{What remains wrong}
The parent universe is a proxy. The optimised index is defined over its
provider's broad investable-market parent of roughly 2{,}500 names; we use the
500-name index. This is the single largest known contributor to the optimised
replication error and no amount of care elsewhere repairs it. Industries are
SIC-derived and therefore coarser and older than the classification the
provider uses, and EDGAR publishes one current code per registrant rather than
a history, so a company that changed business carries its present code
backwards. Section~\ref{sec:limits} returns to both.
